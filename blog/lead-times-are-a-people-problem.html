Lead Times Are A People Problem

Transformer, switchgear, and foundry plants are ramping now for 2027 and 2028 delivery. The bottleneck underneath the equipment story is leadership nobody is hiring for yet.

Lead times are long because supplier capacity is short. Supplier capacity is short partly because of people.

That sentence gets skipped over in most conversations about the current industrial buildout. Procurement teams track lead times. Operations teams track backlog. Almost nobody connects the two numbers back to the hiring problem sitting underneath both of them.

Here is the shape of it. Transformer, switchgear, and foundry capacity is the bottleneck behind grid modernization, data center power buildout, and a wave of industrial reshoring, and new plants and expansions are coming online through 2027 and 2028 to meet that demand. Order backlogs at major electrification and automation suppliers are already sitting at record levels, with orders up double digits year over year and full year guidance still climbing. Every one of those facilities is either brand new or ramping hard, on a timeline set by contracts that were signed years before the equipment ships.

A new or expanding plant does not ramp itself. It needs a full operations leadership team in place before the first unit rolls off the line. That team looks the same across transformer, switchgear, and foundry operations. A plant manager who can run the site. An operations leader who owns throughput against the ramp curve. Maintenance and reliability leads who keep the equipment running at the pace the schedule requires. Quality engineers who catch defects before they become a recall or a rejected shipment. Supply chain executives who manage the inbound side of the exact same capacity crunch the plant was built to solve.

That is five distinct leadership functions, all needed at once, all competing for the same narrow pool of people who have run a plant through a ramp before. This is not a shop floor staffing problem. Shop floor roles backfill faster and from a wider pool. Leadership roles do not. There is no fast substitute for someone who has run a ramp before, managed a quality system under real pressure, or kept a maintenance program ahead of an equipment schedule.

This is where the veteran talent pool fits, not as a mission statement but as a direct functional match. A JMO who ran a unit through a fielding timeline has run a ramp. A technical Staff NCO who owned a maintenance program under operational tempo has managed reliability under pressure the same way a plant does. A logistics or supply officer has handled the exact kind of layered sourcing problem, several vendors and several constraints at once, that a supply chain executive is dealing with right now. A quality assurance background from a program with zero tolerance for defects translates directly into a quality engineering seat that cannot afford to guess. None of that requires translation on faith.

The industry is currently treating the transformer and switchgear shortage as a materials and equipment story. More capacity, more capital, more supplier lines. All of that is true and none of it is sufficient. A new production line without the leadership to run it does not produce faster. It produces later, with more defects, staffed by whoever was available instead of whoever was right for the seat.

This is not a distant problem. The plants driving this window are being planned and staffed right now, with output targets set for 2027 and 2028 already built into contracts signed today. A leadership search for a role like this typically runs several months from opened requisition to a hire. Anyone waiting for the ramp to start before beginning that search is already behind the timeline. The hiring decision and the capital decision need to happen on the same calendar, not one after the other.

The lead time number everyone is watching is downstream of a hiring number nobody is watching closely enough. The next question is what that leadership vacancy costs while it sits open during a ramp that was never designed to wait. That is the math worth running before the next capacity review, not after.

If a market shift is about to change your hiring plan and the pipeline is not built for it yet, we would rather spend 20 minutes understanding the situation than send you a stack of resumes that will not work out.
