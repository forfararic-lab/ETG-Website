Candidate Truth August 2026

Do You Know What You Need to Make That Hire Work?

You have an opening. A director left, or you're scaling and need a head of supply chain. So you hire. You write a job description, run some assessments, interview candidates. The person you select scores well. Then they leave at 18 months or 3 years, and you're hiring again. You think the assessment missed something. It probably did. But that's not the diagnosis. The real question is: do you know what you need to make that hire successful in the context of your business?

Most companies see three things when they face a failed hire: an open position, high turnover in that role, and an assessment that didn't predict success. They think the problem is assessment quality, or candidate quality, or company culture. So they invest in better soft skills assessments, culture training, and consulting. That's logical. It's also backward.

The problem isn't always in processes and culture. The problem is that you haven't defined what success looks like for this role.

You can't write a meaningful job description without it. You can't design an interview process without it. You can't assess for fit without knowing what you're assessing fit toward. And you definitely can't expect someone to succeed in a role if the organization hasn't clarified what success means.

Here's what most companies do instead: they copy a job description from the last person in the role. Or they use a template. They post it. They interview based on the description. They hire someone who matches the words on the page. Then they measure success by whether the person stayed or whether early performance reviews looked good. Neither of those is the real test.

Real success is outcome-based. It's specific. It answers a question in the context of where your business is at and where your business is going.

Does your plant need to stabilize operations for 18 months while you transition leadership? That's the success metric. Not "find a director with X, Y, Z experience." Not "hire someone who fits our culture." Stabilize operations for 18 months. Everything else follows from that.

Does your supply chain function need to be built from scratch? That's success. Not "find someone who collaborates well." Not "hire a strategic thinker." Define and build the function. Now you know what to look for, what trade-offs matter, and what tenure works to support something that could take six months or three years.

Does your sales leader need to turn around a region in 24 months and then scale to VP? That's success. Not "find someone who will grow with us." Deliver specific revenue turnaround in a specific timeframe. Now you know whether someone's profile fits the need.

The problem with assessments isn't their quality. It's that they're measuring fit against an undefined target.

You run a soft skills assessment and it tells you someone scores high on collaborative leadership, strategic thinking, and accountability. Great. But collaborative leadership fit for what? Building a stable function? Navigating chaos? Fixing politics? Strategic thinking toward what outcome? These are different competency profiles, and the assessment can't distinguish between them because you haven't told it what success actually is.

Here's the diagnostic process companies should run before they ever think about assessments:

1. What outcome does this role need to deliver? Not job duties. Outcome. "Stabilize plant operations" or "reduce supply chain costs by 15%" or "build a sales team that can scale to three regions." Be specific and why.

2. What timeline makes sense for that outcome? 18 months? Three years? Five years? The timeline shapes everything about what you're hiring for. A three-month turnaround hire is fundamentally different from a five-year building hire.

3. What has to be true about your environment for someone to deliver that? Does your CEO need to stay out of daily decisions? Does the budget need to be protected? Does the team need to stay stable? Does the manager above need to have real authority? If these things aren't true, no hire survives. This is your diagnosis.

4. What does the person need to succeed in that environment? Growth opportunity? Comp parity with the market? Clear advancement path? Autonomy over specific decisions? Visibility to leadership? Be honest about what you can actually provide.

5. Now, build the assessment around fit to success. Not fit to the job description. Fit to delivering the outcome you defined in environment you actually have.

If you skip steps 1-4 and jump to better assessments, you're measuring fit against a ghost target. You'll have higher-quality data about the wrong problem.

Consider what this looks like in practice. You're hiring a director of operations. Your current assessment looks for collaborative leadership and strategic thinking. Those are reasonable signals. But what are they actually testing?

If your definition of success is "stabilize operations for 18 months while we figure out our next strategy," collaborative leadership matters in a specific way: the director needs to calm a scared team and deliver predictable results. Strategic thinking matters differently: they need to maintain current operations, not redesign the entire system. Autonomy matters because you're in transition. Advancement path doesn't matter because the role may change in 18 months.

Now your assessment changes. You're not testing for "strategic thinker." You're testing for someone who can stabilize under uncertainty. You're not testing for "collaborative leader." You're testing for someone who can build trust with a team that's been burned. These are different signals.

If you hadn't defined success first, you'd hire someone who tests high on strategy and collaboration but freezes when the environment is chaotic. They'd leave at month 10. You'd blame the assessment. The real problem is that success and the hire were never aligned.

Most companies don't do this work. It's harder than writing a job description or running an assessment. It requires your CEO, your operations leader, and your HR leader to sit down and actually agree on what success means. It requires saying no to some candidates who look good on paper but don't fit the actual need. It requires honesty about what your environment can actually provide.

But it's the only way an assessment becomes useful. And it's the only way a hire has a chance to actually work.

Start there. Define success. Then design the role around success. Then build the assessment around fit to success. Everything else—the job description, the interview questions, the compensation structure—follows from that clarity.

If you can't define success, an assessment won't help you. A better job description won't help you. You're hiring against a moving target. And you'll keep seeing the same pattern: strong hires who leave or underperform because they were never set up to succeed in the first place.
