What Is Underneath It

A Microsoft outage across 74 countries and a nationwide FAA ground stop ran the same test. The organizations that held had process, procedure, and people underneath the system.

Every system goes down eventually.

The question is what is underneath it when it does.

Most companies never ask. The system works, so the system becomes the plan. Email is how things get confirmed. The network is how the floor talks to the front office. The schedule in the software is the schedule. People stopped writing down what happens without it, because they haven't needed to.

Earlier this month, a lot of organizations found out at the same time.

On November 12, Microsoft's email and collaboration tools went down across 74 countries. Banks, courts, and government agencies lost the system they use to confirm almost everything. The next afternoon, router work at a remote radio site in El Dorado, Arkansas destabilized the FAA's network, and every departing flight in the country stopped. More than 12,000 flights were canceled in the days that followed. Running under both, the Strait of Hormuz, which the International Energy Agency now calls the largest threat to energy security in history.

Different failures. Same test.

The organizations that held had three things underneath. Process. Procedure. People.

Process first.

The router's supplier says the device did not fail. It did what it was configured to do. Investigators have not settled whether the cause was the hardware, the installation, or an aging network that could not absorb the change. Either way, one change at one site reached across a country, and the network came back when someone made the call to shut that site down.

That is process. Who approves a change. What the rollback is. Who has the authority to pull it, and whether they know they have it. Process does not prevent every failure. It decides how far one travels.

Then procedure.

While the network was unstable, the FAA says controllers and pilots kept following established safety protocols. Nobody improvised the safety of the airspace.

During the Microsoft outage, trading desks in Taiwan and Japan confirmed trades by fax. That was not nostalgia. Someone had kept a backup path, knew where the machine was, and knew who was on the other end.

The Marine Corps never trusted a single path for anything that mattered. Every comm plan had a primary, an alternate, and a tertiary. Underneath all of it sat the oldest fallback there is. A runner.

Most units will never need a runner. It stays in the plan anyway, because the day you need it is not the day to figure it out.

Then people.

Paper and technology cannot be held accountable. A court technology consultant told Axios that courts typically keep manual backup plans, and that the people who staff them can figure out how to do things right.

That second half is the part that matters.

The Marine Corps had an answer for that too. The comm plan covered how to talk when the radios failed. Commander's intent covered how to make decisions in a fluid and information light environment. Every order carried the purpose behind it, so a squad leader could adjust and execute without waiting to be told.

The backup plan kept the message moving. The intent kept the unit moving.

Picture a plant when the MES drops at 2 a.m. One crew stops the line and waits for IT to call back. Another pulls the paper travelers, logs downtime by hand, holds what needs holding, and keeps running. Same software. Same outage. Completely different night.

Both plants might have the same procedure in the same binder. Only one put a shift lead on the floor who knew why it mattered and would act on it.

That is a hiring decision. It is also the one you cannot test in an interview loop. You find out on the night the system fails.

None of this is an argument against technology. Microsoft will fix its email. The FAA will modernize its network. The tools will keep getting better.

But every system goes down eventually. The question is what is underneath it when it does.

For most companies, the honest answer is a person. The only question left is whether you chose that person on purpose.
