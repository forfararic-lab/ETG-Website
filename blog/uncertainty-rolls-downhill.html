Uncertainty Rolls Downhill

Uncertainty picks up weight at every layer it passes through, and it lands on the managers in the middle. This year, a lot of companies cut them.

At the top of a company, uncertainty looks like a planning exercise. Three scenarios. Probabilities attached. Options in each one. The people in that room know what they do not know, and why.

One level down, a decision arrives without the reasoning behind it. Hold hiring. Shift volume to the second line. Push the expansion a quarter.

At the bottom, it is just a question. Is this line still running in March. Is my job safe. Why did the priorities change again.

Uncertainty rolls downhill. And like anything rolling downhill, it picks up weight on the way.

Every layer it passes through strips out context and adds anxiety. Nobody does it on purpose. The decision fits in a five minute stand up. The scenarios, the tradeoffs, and the reasons stay in the room where they were discussed.

So where does the weight land?

Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace report answers that. Between 2022 and 2025, manager engagement fell from 31% to 22%. Non-manager engagement barely moved, from 20% to 19%.

The hit landed in the middle.

That makes sense once you picture the hill. Managers catch it from both directions. Unresolved decisions come down from above. Unanswered questions come up from below. They are the layer expected to turn uncertainty into something a team can act on, usually with less information than anyone assumes they have.

Now the hill is getting steeper.

Korn Ferry's Workforce 2026 report, a survey of more than 16,000 professionals across 11 markets, found that 42% of organizations cut management roles over the past year. More than half of the managers who remain, 55%, say they are exhausted. And 39% of workers say the lack of managers leaves them feeling directionless.

Directionless is the right word. It is what the bottom of the hill feels like when nobody is standing in the middle.

Fewer catch points means more weight on each one that remains. Gallup estimates managers account for at least 70% of the variance in employee engagement. Remove a manager and that variance does not disappear. It lands on the team, unfiltered.

A lot of what companies are doing right now assumes this passes. Hold the decision until things settle. Cut a layer until the outlook clears. Wait for a calm that is not on any forecast.

The military never had that option. Uncertainty was not a phase to wait out. It was the operating condition, so the system was built to work inside it. The answer is called commander's intent.

Every order carries the purpose behind it and the end state it is trying to reach, not just the tasks. When the plan breaks, and it will, a squad leader who understands the purpose can make the right call without waiting for new orders. The plan is expected to fail. The intent is built to survive it.

Commander's intent does not remove uncertainty. It stops uncertainty from gaining weight on the way down. And it does something more useful than that. It lets a unit move while the other side is still waiting for clarity.

Industrial operations need this more than most. In Korn Ferry's industrial cut of the same survey, fewer than half of industrial workers said they feel encouraged to try new ideas, and half said they are not getting the training they need for automation and AI. Dave Rossi, who leads Korn Ferry's global industrial market, puts the burden on leaders to explain how today's work will change over time. That is commander's intent in civilian language.

In a plant, it sounds like this. Commercial volume may slip next quarter. The defense contract delivery does not. If you have to choose, protect the delivery. Three sentences. It gives a supervisor something to decide with the day the schedule changes again, instead of a question to escalate and wait on.

Without it, the supervisor gets the decision, the crew gets the question, and the weight keeps building until someone decides the easiest way out of the uncertainty is out of the building.

Uncertainty does not hit every company the same way. It separates them. The ones with someone standing in the middle keep moving while the rest wait for a calm that is not coming.

What a company controls is where the uncertainty stops, and whether anyone is still standing there to stop it.

If a market shift is about to change your hiring plan and the pipeline is not built for it yet, we would rather spend 20 minutes understanding the situation than send you a stack of resumes that will not work out.
