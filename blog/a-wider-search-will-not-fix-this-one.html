Industry POV October 2026

A Wider Search Will Not Fix This One

Job cuts are down. Hiring plans are up. The labor force is still shrinking. That is not a demand problem, and it is not fixed by searching wider.

I wrote about the same five resumes problem before. Four agencies, one role, the same five candidates under four different letterheads, because every firm is running the same keyword search against the same shallow pool. That is still true. It is also no longer the only reason this keeps happening.

The US labor force has shrunk by roughly 700,000 people so far this year. Outside of a recession, that has only happened once since 1948.

This is not a story about weak demand. Challenger, Gray and Christmas reported job cuts down 46 percent year over year in July, and employer hiring plans up 25 percent year to date. Companies are not pulling back. They are trying to hire into a pool that keeps getting smaller.

That changes the diagnosis. The first version of the same five resumes problem was a search quality problem. Fix the search, widen the net past the keyword veteran, and you find people the other three agencies missed. That fix still works today, and I still believe in it.

But there is a second version now, and it does not respond to a wider search. When the labor force itself contracts, the number of people qualified for a given role shrinks along with it. At some point, five firms running five searches do not find five different slates. They find the same five names, not because they all searched lazily, but because five names may be all there is.

This hits industrial, defense, and energy employers harder than most sectors, for a simple reason. These roles need specific technical backgrounds. A plant manager cannot take a close enough candidate the way a generalist corporate role sometimes can. When the supply of genuinely qualified people shrinks, the gap between a good hire and a bad one gets wider, not narrower, at exactly the moment a company has the least room to absorb a bad one.

Watch what happens next in industrial and defense hiring specifically. Plant managers and TA leaders who are used to forty qualified resumes for one requisition are about to get four. The firms still running the volume play will keep sending back the same names, and for the first time, wider searching alone will not change the outcome.

This is exactly the environment where a narrow, deliberate search wins. Not because it is nicer. Because it is the only approach built to work when the pool is small and the fit has to be right the first time, and because it forces an honest answer to a harder question. Is this one of the five, or is this someone four other firms have not thought to look for yet.

Saying that out loud means telling a client the uncomfortable version of the truth. Here is who is available right now. Here is what it will take to get them. And here is when the right answer is to wait, or widen the role requirements, rather than force a hire that will not last ninety days. It is hard to say that part, because it slows down the fee, and it slows down growth and revenue production, and admitting the pool is smaller than it used to be means admitting their old process no longer works.

If the labor force keeps contracting into next year, and there is no clear signal yet that it will reverse, the hiring playbook built for an abundant labor market breaks. Not gradually. All at once, the first time a client needs five hires out of a pool that only has five qualified candidates to begin with, and every other firm sends back the same five names, this time for a reason no wider search can solve.

The firms that adapt first will be the ones who can tell the difference between the two problems, and say so out loud.

If a market shift is about to change your hiring plan and the pipeline is not built for it yet, we would rather spend 20 minutes understanding the situation than send you a stack of resumes that will not work out.
