The Honest Assessment November 2026

What Does the Seat Need?

A tight market pushes speed up and fit down. When a label does the work an assessment should, a reliable hire ends up in a seat built for someone else.

In September, US manufacturers had 584,000 open jobs and made 313,000 hires.

A year earlier, openings sat at 389,000. The gap between what plants need and what they can fill has rarely been wider.

When the gap gets that wide, something predictable happens. The bar on speed goes up and the bar on fit comes down. A label starts doing the work an assessment should do.

Veteran is one of those labels.

So here is something a firm built around military talent is not supposed to say. Sometimes the veteran is the wrong hire.

Not because of anything about veterans. Because of the seat.

Start with the number everyone in this space quotes. Roughly half of veterans leave their first civilian job within a year. Trace it back and you land on a Syracuse University survey from 2014. Twelve years old, and still showing up in 2026 reports as if it were new.

There is a better number. Research from Penn State's Clearinghouse for Military Family Readiness and LinkedIn puts more than 60 percent of veterans in underemployment, working in roles that do not match their experience, skills, or education.

That is the mismatch most companies never see. It does not look like a bad hire. It looks like a reliable one, sitting in a seat built for someone else.

Fit is the variable. Veteran status is not.

That shows up in a handful of ways that are easy to miss when a req has been open for ninety days.

The title gets hired instead of the function. An infantry officer, a logistics officer, and an aviation maintenance officer all leave with the same rank. They do not leave with the same skills. The logistics officer has spent years running supply, maintenance, and transportation, which is the closest military match to running plant operations. The aviation maintenance officer maps onto MRO. The infantry officer brings leadership under pressure, but usually needs time in a production role before running an operation. Put any of them in the wrong seat and the resume still looks strong. Rank tells you the level. It does not tell you the lane.

The role leads no one. A Captain who led 150 people gets hired into an individual contributor role because the resume looked strong. The work is fine. The job is just not the one they are built for. That hire is likely gone within a year, and it is hard to blame them.

The role carries responsibility with no authority. The job description says own the outcome. The org chart says get approval for everything. Plenty of people struggle in that seat. Someone trained to act on intent with clear lines of authority will usually notice the gap first, and say so.

The job was sold as something it is not. The interview describes a leadership role. The reality is firefighting under a manager who has never delegated anything. That is a mismatch with any candidate. It just surfaces faster with one who expected the job they were told about.

The company wants the label more than the person. A veteran hiring goal is a fine thing to have. It is a bad reason to fill a specific seat. If the honest answer is that a candidate with fifteen years in your industry is the right hire for this role, a veteran hiring commitment does not change that.

For the veteran, this cuts the other way too. Sometimes the right move is not the seat you look qualified for on paper. It is the one before it.

That is not underemployment. Underemployment is being stuck below your skills with no way up. The job before the one you want, with a real path to the next one, is how you get there faster. RAND tracked post 9/11 veterans through their first eight years in the civilian workforce and found they often start with slightly lower earnings than nonveterans, then grow their earnings faster.

None of this is a reason to stop hiring veterans. It is a reason to know what the seat actually requires before anyone opens a search.

That is the work most hiring skips under pressure. Three questions get most of the way there.

What will this person decide on their own by month three?

Who will they lead, and how much authority will they actually have over them?

Why did the last person in this seat leave or fail?

If the answers describe a role where a junior officer or senior NCO will be trusted, stretched, and accountable, that is a strong case for military talent. If the answers describe something else, the honest move is to say so before the search starts. Even when the person saying it gets paid to find veterans.

That conversation costs something in the short term. It is also the only reason anyone should believe the next recommendation.

A tight market makes every open seat feel urgent. It does not make a bad match a good one. It just makes it a faster one.

If you are trying to figure out whether a gap on your leadership bench is a hiring problem or something else, we would rather spend 20 minutes understanding the situation than send you a stack of resumes that will not work out.
