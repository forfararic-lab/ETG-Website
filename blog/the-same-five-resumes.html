The Same Five Resumes

Four agencies, four fee structures, and the same five resumes under every letterhead. That is not bad luck. That is what a keyword search always produces.

How often have you seen a company running multiple search agencies on the same role?

Different firms, different fee structures, different account managers calling to check in. Four separate pipelines, in theory.

In practice, the same five resumes keep showing up. Different letterhead on the submission email. Same five people underneath it.

That is not bad luck. That is what happens when every agency is searching the same keyword in the same region. Then an agency comes along and pitches veteran talent as the solution. It makes sense. They are geographically mobile, talented, and capable of doing the work.

The problem is that most recruiters find veteran candidates by searching for the word veteran. Military. Prior service. Leadership. Broad labels that pull a generic slice of a much deeper pool, and every firm running the same broad search pulls the same slice. Multiple agencies fishing the same water with the same net will always come up with the same five fish.

Here is what gets missed. The resume does not read the same.

A line that reads managed logistics operations for a battalion could mean coordinating equipment and personnel for a scheduled training rotation. It could also mean rerouting an entire supply chain overnight after the original plan fell apart, in a deployed environment. Both get written in the same six words. Only one of them tells you anything about how that person performs when a plan breaks in real time and nobody is coming to fix it for them.

A line that reads led a team of forty could mean running a shift on a known schedule with a known outcome. It could also mean holding a unit together through equipment failure and a mission that did not go the way it was briefed, and still delivering the outcome anyway. Same four words. Completely different person underneath them, and nothing on the page tells you which one you are looking at.

Getting past the label takes a different kind of search and a different kind of conversation. Reading a DD214 and a set of orders is a start, not an answer. The real work is sitting with someone long enough to walk the line item back to the day it happened, what broke, what the plan was before it broke, what they did in the gap between the two. That conversation takes longer than a keyword match. It also takes longer than most contingency search models are built to spend, which is exactly why the same five names keep resurfacing across four different letterheads.

The claim that veterans make good leaders and good employees only goes so far. It is not useful information to a hiring manager juggling multiple priorities and it is not why a search succeeds or fails. It is knowing what actually happened behind the four words on the page, and whether that experience matches what the role in front of you actually demands.

The agencies sending the same five resumes are not wrong that those five people are strong candidates. They are wrong in saying that they are finding new candidates that have not been spoken to by either the company they are supporting or by other agencies doing the same thing.

That is the difference between a keyword search and an honest assessment. One produces a list. The other produces a person, matched against what the job requires, not what the label suggests. A hiring manager running four pipelines does not need a fifth list. They need someone willing to say which candidate on that list is actually right, and which one just looks right on paper.

If you are trying to figure out what a candidate's military background actually means for the role you are filling, we would rather spend 20 minutes understanding the situation than send you a stack of resumes that will not work out.
